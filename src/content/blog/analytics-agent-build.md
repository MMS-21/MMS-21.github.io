A manager opens an HR dashboard. Headcount is down. Overtime is up. Attrition is red.

The charts describe a problem. They leave the most useful questions unanswered: **Where should we investigate first? What could explain the pattern? What can we change, and how will we know whether it helped?**

When I look at people data, that is the conversation I want the analysis to support. A polished report helps people read the evidence. Its real value comes from helping them make a better decision about work and the people doing it.

This is the checklist I would bring to an HR analysis. The recommendations are my practical synthesis. The linked research provides context, rather than proof that the same explanation applies to every organisation.

## Start with the decision

“Analyse our workforce” is too broad. “Should we change onboarding for new hires in customer support?” gives the analysis a purpose. Agree on the decision, the owner, the population affected, and the time available to act. Ask what evidence could change that person's mind.

The [CIPD's people analytics guide](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/guides/analytics-practitioner-guide/) places organisational data alongside professional expertise, scientific research, and stakeholder views. My interpretation is practical: an HR extract should start a conversation with the people doing the work, not replace it.

## Establish what one row means

An employee record, a monthly snapshot, a payroll transaction, and an exit event are different units of analysis. Joining them carelessly can multiply employees and distort salary averages.

Before calculating anything, check:

- **Coverage:** Does the file include leavers, contractors, part-time staff, and all relevant locations? A current-employee file alone cannot describe historical turnover.
- **History:** Are IDs unique at the intended grain? Do role and manager fields describe the person at the event date or today?
- **Dates:** Are hires, transfers, exits, and reporting periods consistent? Have new-hire cohorts had enough time to complete the retention window?
- **Definitions:** Separate headcount from full-time equivalent, and voluntary exits from dismissals, retirements, and internal moves.
- **Missingness:** Are missing exit reasons or survey responses concentrated in particular teams? Missing feedback does not mean satisfaction.

For period turnover, a common operational definition is exits during the period divided by average headcount during that period. State the population and averaging method. A snapshot's share of records marked “Attrition = Yes” is a different measure and needs a different label.

## Read counts and rates together

Imagine Team A has 20 exits from an average headcount of 200. Team B has 8 exits from an average headcount of 40. These illustrative figures give B a higher turnover rate, 20% versus 10%, while A creates more replacement work.

Both views matter. Show the numerator, denominator, period, and comparison beside the rate. Compare similar roles and tenure groups. Flag small groups where one exit changes the percentage sharply. A company average can improve while a critical team deteriorates.

## Look across the employee journey

Human capital management involves building capability and making work sustainable as well as retaining employees. Choose metrics according to the decision, rather than filling every report with everything available.

| Area | What to investigate | Decision it can support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hiring and onboarding | Vacancy duration, offer acceptance, cohort retention, time to proficiency | Where recruitment or onboarding needs attention |
| Retention | Voluntary exits by role, tenure, location, and critical skill | Which departures threaten capability and where to investigate |
| Workload and wellbeing | Overtime hours, staffing gaps, absence patterns, employee feedback | Whether to review staffing, scheduling, or work design |
| Pay and progression | Pay within comparable jobs, promotion access, internal moves | Where reward or career barriers need investigation |
| Learning | Skill coverage, learning access, demonstrated application | Whether development improves readiness for the work |
| Employee experience | Survey participation, recurring themes, follow-up actions | Which concerns need a response and who will provide it |

Training completion measures activity. Evidence that someone can apply the skill gets closer to an outcome. A low vacancy count can also hide essential roles that have remained unfilled for months.

## An association is the beginning of an investigation

Higher attrition among employees working overtime does not establish that overtime caused the exits. Both could reflect understaffing, difficult assignments, seasonality, or role differences. Departures may also increase overtime for the people who remain.

Compare relevant groups, inspect timing, and bring in staffing history and employee feedback. A snapshot cannot establish the sequence of events. Manager comparisons also need context about team composition and inherited vacancies before assigning responsibility.

## Pay matters. So do everyday working conditions

In [Gallup's research on preventable turnover](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/646538/employee-turnover-preventable-often-ignored.aspx), Corey Tatel and Ben Wigert report that 42% of 717 surveyed voluntary leavers believed their manager or organisation could have done something to prevent their departure. Their discussion covers compensation, relationships, career opportunities, and workload. This is a retrospective employee assessment, not an experimental estimate of how many exits an intervention will prevent.

That perspective suggests useful local questions: Are expectations clear? Can employees see a path forward? Are staffing problems repeatedly left unresolved? Does feedback lead to a response?

Donald Sull, Charles Sull, and Ben Zweig offer another lens. [MIT Sloan Management Review's research announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/toxic-culture-not-wages-is-driving-the-great-resignation-301457839.html) identifies toxic culture as a leading predictor of company-level attrition in its study of large US employers during April to September 2021. That context matters. It is a reason to investigate culture alongside pay, not a universal explanation for individual departures.

For an analyst, the implication is to combine quantitative patterns with carefully collected employee perspectives. Salary and job title alone cannot capture the experience of working in a team.

## Make fairness and privacy part of the work

An average pay gap starts an investigation. Role, level, location, hours, and access to progression need examination. Adjusted comparisons cannot establish fairness by themselves if the processes determining roles and promotions are unequal.

My reporting approach would limit identifiable records, combine or suppress small groups, and protect individual survey answers. Removing names is insufficient when a role and location identify someone. The [CIPD guide](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/guides/analytics-practitioner-guide/) also highlights the need to handle personal information under the requirements applicable to the organisation.

A predictive “flight risk” score should not become an automatic judgement about commitment or a reason to deny opportunities. Reviewing team working conditions often provides a more constructive starting point.

## Give managers a briefing they can act on

I would put six things beside the dashboard:

1. **Finding:** What changed, where, and compared with what?
2. **Significance:** Which work, capability, or employee experience is affected?
3. **Confidence:** What does the evidence support, and what remains unknown?
4. **Next step:** What should we investigate or test first?
5. **Owner and review date:** Who will act, and when will the team revisit it?
6. **Outcome and guardrails:** What improvement would count, and what must not worsen?

An illustrative recommendation: “HR and the support lead will review scheduling and onboarding in the affected team, then pilot a revised approach with the next intake. Review workload and cohort retention once the observation window is complete, alongside service quality and employee feedback.”

That identifies an action and a way to learn from it. A before-and-after improvement still does not prove the pilot caused the change. Consider seasonality, workforce changes, and a suitable comparison where feasible.

## Financial scenarios need visible assumptions

If a report estimates savings from fewer exits, show the assumed reduction, cost per replacement, intervention cost, and time horizon. Separate recruitment costs from estimated lost productivity. Use ranges when inputs are uncertain.

A scenario helps compare options. Correct arithmetic alone does not make it a measured saving or validated forecast.

## The question I want the report to answer

**What should we do next, for whom, and what evidence would tell us to continue or change course?**

This connects analysis to management decisions and employee experience. It also allows an honest answer when evidence is incomplete: here is the pattern, here is what we cannot explain, and here is the next investigation worth doing.
